Go The flash leaves Earth

Light covers 299,792 kilometres every second. The green ring is how far the flash has got, in every direction at once. The straight line is the part of it aimed at Voyager 1.

The clock is how long it has been travelling. Scrolling is the only thing that moves it.

1.3 s The Moon

The flash is as far away as the Moon: 384,400 kilometres.

Radio travels at the same speed, so for the Apollo crews every word from Houston arrived this late.

8 min 19 s As far as the Sun

Sunlight makes this trip the other way all day long. The Sun you see is the Sun as it was eight minutes ago.

Astronomers call this distance one astronomical unit, or AU: about 150 million kilometres. The rest of the journey is measured in it.

12 min 38 s As far out as Mars

A yardstick, not a meeting. The flash is climbing out of the flat plane the planets move in, so it passes none of them.

From here each ring marks how far a planet’s orbit lies from the Sun. Mars: about one and a half times as far as Earth.

43 min 15 s Jupiter’s orbit

Voyager 1 needed eighteen months to get this far. It flew past Jupiter in March 1979 and found volcanoes erupting on its moon Io.

1 h 19 min Saturn’s orbit

Voyager 1 passed Saturn in November 1980, swung close to its moon Titan, and was thrown up and out of the plane of the planets.

It has not been near anything since.

2 h 40 min Uranus’s orbit

Twice as far out as Saturn. Only one spacecraft has ever visited Uranus: Voyager 2, in January 1986.

4 h 10 min Neptune’s orbit

The last planet. Voyager 2 came by in August 1989, twelve years after it set out. Light does it in an afternoon.

Every planet is now behind the flash, and the day is about one sixth gone.

5 h 29 min Pluto’s distance

Pluto’s average distance from the Sun, in the middle of the Kuiper belt: a wide ring of ice and rock left over from when the planets formed.

Now the picture pulls back. Everything so far fits in the first quarter of the day.

8 h The long dark

From here the page is to scale. Every hour of light-time is the same length of scrolling, and there is nothing to put in most of them.

That is the point.

13 h 2 min The termination shock

The Sun blows a thin wind of charged particles in every direction at more than a million kilometres an hour. Here it slows suddenly, as it starts to feel the gas between the stars pushing back.

Voyager 1 crossed this line in December 2004, 94 AU out.

16 h 55 min The edge of the Sun’s bubble

The heliopause: where the Sun’s wind stops and the space between the stars begins. Voyager 1 crossed it on 25 August 2012, about 122 AU out. Nothing made by people had left the bubble before.

It is not the edge of the solar system. The Sun’s gravity holds comets far beyond here.

20 h 6 min As far as Voyager 2

Voyager 2 is about this far from Earth, roughly 145 AU, but off in another direction: it is heading south of the planets while Voyager 1 heads north.

It should reach its own light-day in the middle of the 2030s.

24 h Voyager 1

One light-day: 25,902,068,371 kilometres. The flash has caught a machine the size of a small car that left Earth on 5 September 1977.

Light took a day. Voyager 1 took forty-nine years.

What a day buys

  • 49 yearsHow long Voyager 1 took to cover what the flash did in a day.
  • 2 daysTo ask Voyager 1 a question and hear the answer: a day out, a day back.
  • 17,500 timesHow much faster the flash is than the spacecraft, which does about 17 kilometres a second.
  • 48 yearsUntil Voyager 1 is two light-days out, at the speed it is going now.
  • 20 hoursNear enough. The stretch of the day with almost nothing in it: everything after Neptune’s orbit, 19 hours 50 minutes.
  • 1,550 daysHow long the same flash would need to reach the nearest star, Proxima Centauri. That is four and a quarter years.

How the figures were worked out

  • The speed of light, 299,792.458 kilometres a second, and the astronomical unit, 149,597,870.7 kilometres, are exact by definition. Every light-time on this page is a distance divided by that speed.
  • The Moon’s distance is its average, 384,400 kilometres. The planets’ distances are their average distances from the Sun, rounded, used as yardsticks. The flash is heading about 35 degrees north of the plane of the planets, towards where Voyager 1 is, and does not pass them.
  • The picture is a diagram. Distances along the line are true to the scale shown at that moment. Directions are not: the Moon and the Sun are drawn at the right distance but not in the right part of the sky.
  • The date, 18 November 2026, is the one NASA’s Voyager team gave for the light-day, as reported in the press. Voyager 1’s distance from Earth also rises and falls a little each year as Earth goes round the Sun.
  • The crossings of the termination shock (December 2004, 94 AU) and the heliopause (25 August 2012, about 122 AU) are NASA’s figures for Voyager 1. Voyager 2’s distance, about 145 AU, is an estimate for November 2026 and is the least certain number here.
  • Voyager 1 is leaving at about 3.6 AU a year, which is where the 17 kilometres a second, the 17,500 times and the 48 years come from.
