The job that said nothing

Some jobs fail loudly. The dangerous ones run on time, report success and do nothing. A note on silence, and how to hear it.

A failure makes a noise. Something stops, a light goes red, somebody swears. You know where to look, and usually when. Most of what we build to watch our machines is built to hear that noise.

The failure worth fearing makes no noise at all. It is the task that starts on time, runs to the end, reports success and does no work. Nothing stops. No light changes. The first sign is a person asking, weeks later, why the thing they rely on has had nothing new in it since the spring.

01The dog in the night

The best-known lesson in this is a dog that did not bark. In Arthur Conan Doyle’s “Silver Blaze”, a racehorse is taken from its stable at night, and the stable dog stays quiet.1 Holmes takes the silence for the clue: the dog knew the visitor. Everyone else had looked at what happened. He asked what should have happened, and had not.

Absence is evidence only to someone who expected a presence. A bark announces itself. A missing bark has to be noticed by somebody who was listening for it, at the right hour, with an idea already in mind of what the night ought to sound like.

02Three outcomes, not two

We talk about jobs as if they had two outcomes, and they have three. It worked. It failed. Or it ran and did nothing. From outside, the third looks exactly like the first.

Zero rows read, zero files written, zero messages sent: all done without complaint.

The usual check asks only whether the job finished. A program ends by handing back a number, and zero means that it ended without complaint.2 That is a statement about the ending. It says nothing about the middle.

A job can read nothing, write nothing and send nothing, and still hand back a zero. Zero rows read, zero files written, zero messages sent: all done without complaint. The number is honest. We are the ones who read “finished” and heard “worked”.

03Why silence wins

Silence wins because it is the cheapest thing a system can do. A failure takes effort: something has to notice, stop, and say so. Doing nothing takes none. A source moves, a filter grows too strict, a list comes back empty, and every step after that is handed nothing, does nothing with it and passes nothing on, correctly.

It also wins because nobody reads good news. A column of green ticks is looked at once, on the day it is built. After that it is furniture. Our own dashboards train us to look only when something is red, and silence is never red.

04Nine quiet nights

Take a made-up case of a familiar kind: a nightly news job whose feed moves, so that it reads an empty list and reports success. The job gathers news each night for a morning briefing. One night the site it reads moves its feed to a new address. The old address still answers, politely, with an empty list. The job finds no stories, writes a briefing with no stories in it, and says that all is well.

The job does this for nine nights before anyone asks. Nobody is told, because nothing failed. The briefing looks thin, and then it looks normal, because thin is what it has become. On the tenth day somebody asks whether there has really been no news.

05How to hear it

The cure is to make every job say what it did, in numbers, every time. Not “done”. Say “read 212 items, kept 31, wrote one file with 31 stories in it”. A line like that can be wrong in ways that show.

Then decide, in advance, what too little looks like. Thirty-one stories is a normal night. Three is odd. None is a fault, whatever number the program hands back. Write the floor down where the job can check itself against it, and have it complain when it falls through.

And arrange to be told when nothing arrives at all. Railways dealt with this long ago, with a handle the driver has to keep hold of: let go, and the train stops.3 The software version is a message that has to keep arriving. The alarm is its absence.

Three habits

  1. Every run says what it did, in numbers.
  2. Every job knows what too little looks like, and complains.
  3. Something notices when a message that should arrive does not.

06What it costs

None of this is expensive. One line of output, one number to hold it against, one timer. The cost is in the habit: somebody has to decide what normal is, and mind when it changes.

The habit of asking what a job made is worth more than any tool. A team that asks “what did it actually make?” of every success will find its silent jobs early. A team that asks only “did it pass?” will find them when a reader does.

So listen for the dog. When something that ought to make a noise has made none, that is not peace. Go and look at the stable.
