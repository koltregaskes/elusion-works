The Shipping Forecast, examined

Thirty‑one
seas.

Four times a day, a calm voice reads the names of the waters around Britain. Most listeners will never see Dogger or Fisher. They tune in anyway.

Words by Agnes Penrose · Chart by the Longwave desk · 9 min read

The bulletin begins the same way every time: the time it was issued, the gale warnings, a general synopsis, and then the areas, in a fixed order that runs clockwise from Viking in the far north, down the east coast, along the Channel, out into the Atlantic and back up to Southeast Iceland.

It exists because of a shipwreck. After the Royal Charter went down off Anglesey in the storm of 1859, Vice-Admiral Robert FitzRoy pushed for a warning service, and from 1861 storm signals were sent by telegraph to ports around the coast. The idea that weather could be forecast at all was controversial. FitzRoy coined the word for it.

Viking, North Utsire, South Utsire, Forties.The opening four, as most of Britain knows them

The BBC first broadcast forecasts for shipping in the 1920s. The format has been tuned since, but not by much. Each area gets wind direction and force, then weather, then visibility, and almost never a word more. Good, moderate, poor: the vocabulary is small enough to survive a bad signal on a small boat.

The map changes slowly. Finisterre became FitzRoy in 2002, partly to avoid confusion with a Spanish area of the same name, and partly as a tribute. Trafalgar is only read in the late broadcast, after Sailing By has played the night in.

Ask people why they listen and you rarely hear about the weather. They talk about the rhythm, the names, the sense that somebody is keeping watch. It is a public service that turns out to be poetry, by accident and by discipline.
