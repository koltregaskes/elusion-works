Go The flash leaves Earth

Light covers 299,792 kilometres every second. The green ring is how far the flash has got, in every direction at once. The straight line is the part of it aimed at Voyager 1.

The picture is a diagram, not a map. Every ring is a distance from Earth, nothing is drawn in its true direction, and everything is held still while the flash travels.

1.28 s The Moon

The flash is as far away as the Moon: 384,400 kilometres.

Radio travels at the same speed, so for the Apollo crews every word from Houston arrived this late.

8 min 19 s As far as the Sun

Sunlight makes this trip the other way all day long. The Sun you see is the Sun as it was eight minutes ago.

Astronomers call this distance one astronomical unit, or AU: about 150 million kilometres. The rest of the journey is measured in it.

The page is squeezed here: about a screen of scrolling for each mark, however long light takes between them. After six hours it is to scale.

12 min 40 s As far out as Mars

A yardstick, not a meeting. The flash is climbing out of the flat plane the planets move in, so it passes none of them.

From here each ring is named for a planet whose orbit lies that far from the Sun. Mars: about one and a half times as far out as Earth.

43 min 14 s As far out as Jupiter

Voyager 1 needed eighteen months to get this far. It flew past Jupiter in March 1979 and found volcanoes erupting on its moon Io.

1 h 19 min As far out as Saturn

Voyager 1 passed Saturn in November 1980, swung close to its moon Titan, and was thrown up and out of the plane of the planets.

It has not been near anything since.

2 h 39 min As far out as Uranus

Twice as far out as Saturn. Only one spacecraft has ever visited Uranus: Voyager 2, in January 1986.

4 h 10 min As far out as Neptune

The last planet. Voyager 2 came by in August 1989, twelve years after it set out. Light does it in an afternoon.

The flash is now further out than any planet, and the day is about one sixth gone.

5 h 28 min Pluto’s distance

Pluto’s average distance from the Sun, in the middle of the Kuiper belt: a wide ring of ice and rock left over from when the planets formed.

Now the picture pulls back. Everything so far fits in the first quarter of the day.

8 h The long dark

From here the page is to scale. Every hour of light-time is the same length of scrolling, and there is nothing to put in most of them.

That is the point.

13 h 1 min The termination shock

The Sun blows a thin wind of charged particles in every direction at more than a million kilometres an hour. Here it slows suddenly, as it starts to feel the gas between the stars pushing back.

Voyager 1 crossed this line in December 2004, when it was 94 AU from the Sun.

16 h 54 min The edge of the Sun’s bubble

The heliopause: where the Sun’s wind stops and the space between the stars begins. Voyager 1 crossed it on 25 August 2012, about 122 AU from the Sun. Nothing made by people had left the bubble before.

It is not the edge of the solar system. The Sun’s gravity holds comets far beyond here.

20 h 5 min As far as Voyager 2

Voyager 2 is about this far from Earth, roughly 145 AU, but off in another direction: it is heading south of the planets while Voyager 1 heads north.

It should reach its own light-day in the middle of the 2030s.

24 h Voyager 1

One light-day: 25,902,068,371 kilometres. The flash has caught a machine the size of a small car that left Earth on 5 September 1977.

Light took a day. Voyager 1 took forty-nine years.

What a day buys

  • 49 yearsHow long Voyager 1 took to cover what the flash did in a day.
  • 2 daysTo ask Voyager 1 a question and hear the answer: a day out, a day back.
  • 17,600 timesRoughly how much faster the flash is than the spacecraft, which does about 17 kilometres a second.
  • 48 yearsUntil Voyager 1 is two light-days out, at the speed it is going now.
  • 20 hoursNear enough. The stretch of the day with almost nothing in it: everything further out than Neptune, 19 hours 49 minutes.
  • 1,550 daysHow long the same flash would need to reach the nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri. That is four and a quarter years.

How the figures were worked out

  • The speed of light, 299,792.458 kilometres a second, and the astronomical unit, 149,597,870.7 kilometres, are exact by definition. Every light-time on this page is a distance divided by that speed.
  • The Moon’s distance is its average, 384,400 kilometres. The planets’ distances are their average distances from the Sun (1.524, 5.2, 9.54, 19.2, 30.1 and 39.5 AU), used as yardsticks. The flash is heading about 35 degrees north of the plane of the planets, towards where Voyager 1 is, and does not pass them.
  • The picture is a diagram. Distances along the line are true to its scale at that moment, and the length of the line as drawn is written beside it: at the end, one light-day. Directions are not true: the Moon and the Sun are drawn at the right distance but not in the right part of the sky.
  • For the first six hours of light-time the page is squeezed: each stretch between two marks gets about a screen. After six hours every hour is the same length of scrolling. The bar marked “The day, to scale” is true throughout.
  • Times on the cards are cut short, not rounded up, so a card never reads later than the clock beside it.
  • The date, 18 November 2026, is the one NASA’s Voyager team gave for the light-day, as reported in the press. An outside review of this page says NASA’s own website gives the same date; the session that built the page could not open that site to check. Voyager 1’s distance from Earth also rises and falls a little each year as Earth goes round the Sun.
  • The crossings of the termination shock (December 2004, 94 AU) and the heliopause (25 August 2012, about 122 AU) are NASA’s figures for Voyager 1, measured from the Sun at the time it crossed. They are laid along the line as yardsticks, like the planets. Voyager 2’s distance, about 145 AU, is an estimate for November 2026 and is the least certain number here.
  • Voyager 1 is leaving at about 3.6 AU a year, which is where the 17 kilometres a second, the 17,600 times and the 48 years come from.
