How it works ↓
A field guide in eight labs

How do you stop a river with concrete?

Hoover Dam holds back the Colorado River in Black Canyon on the Nevada–Arizona border. Behind it, Lake Mead can store about two years of the river's flow. In front, seventeen turbines turn falling water into electricity. Above, every bit of it is live: the same model drives the 3D canyon, the gauges and the labs below. Change something and watch the numbers follow.

Lab 01 · Holding the load

Water pushes hardest at the bottom

Water pressure grows steadily with depth: about 1 psi for every 2.31 ft. At the foot of a full Lake Mead the water pushes on the dam at over 300 psi. That's why the dam is 45 ft thick at the crest and 660 ft at the base.

A gravity dam must not slide or tip over. Water seeping under it also pushes up (uplift), making the dam effectively lighter. Hoover's grout curtain and drainage holes cut that uplift. Drag the probe, thin the base, switch off the drains and watch the safety factors.

    Lab 02 · Why it curves

    An arch turns push into squeeze

    Seen from above, Hoover bows upstream like a bridge laid on its side. Water presses on the convex face; the arch carries that load around its curve and into the canyon walls as compression, which concrete handles superbly.

    For a ring under pressure, the squeeze in the concrete is roughly σ = p·R / t. A tighter curve (smaller R) means less stress for the same thickness. But a straight wall gets no arch help at all, so it has to rely on weight alone. Hoover splits the work: designers used the trial-load method to share the load between horizontal arches and vertical cantilevers.

    Lab 03 · Water to wire

    One Francis turbine, opened up

    Water from the penstock fills a snail-shaped spiral case, is aimed by fixed stay vanes, then metered by movable wicket gates onto the runner. It leaves through the draft tube, which slows it down to recover a little more energy.

    The runner drives a generator on the same shaft. To make 60 Hz at 180 rpm the rotor needs 40 poles: f = poles × rpm ÷ 120. The governor holds that speed exactly while the wicket gates open and close to follow the grid.

    Lab 04 · The heat problem

    A dam that would take a century to cool

    Cement gives off heat as it cures. Poured as one block, Hoover's concrete would have stayed hot for more than a century, then cracked as it cooled and shrank.

    So it was built as interlocking columns, poured in 5 ft lifts, threaded with about 582 miles of 1 inch steel pipe. River water ran through first, then water chilled by a refrigeration plant on site. When a column had cooled and shrunk, the joints were filled with grout to lock the dam into one piece.

    The model here is simple heat diffusion: cooling time grows with the square of the distance heat has to travel. Halve the spacing, quarter the wait.

      Lab 06 · The lake's ledger

      Lake Mead is a bank account for water

      Every day: change in storage = inflow − releases − evaporation. Lake Mead loses roughly 600,000 acre-feet a year to evaporation alone. Since 2000 a long drought and heavy demand have drawn it down far below full, leaving the white bathtub ring.

      Lower water means less head, so the same release makes less electricity. At about 950 ft the turbines must stop; at 895 ft, dead pool, no water can leave the dam by gravity.

      About this model

      The 3D canyon is generated from code, not survey data, and is shaped to Black Canyon's proportions around the real dam dimensions. The reservoir and powerplant model is a teaching simplification: lake storage follows an approximate area–capacity curve, turbines follow P = η ρ g Q H with a simple efficiency curve and a 122 MW cap per unit (2,080 MW nameplate shared across 17), spillways follow the weir equation Q = C L h1.5 with the real gate lengths, and tailwater is held near Lake Mohave's level. Numbers are good to within the right ballpark, not for operations.
